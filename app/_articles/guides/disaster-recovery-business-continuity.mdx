## Continuity Starts With Doors

A smart lock disaster recovery plan is not mainly about the app or the cloud account. It is about keeping the right doors usable when one part of the access system fails. A dead battery, offline hub, disabled phone, vendor outage, staff turnover, or broken automation can all interrupt entry in different ways. The plan should say what still works in each case.

For a home, continuity may be as simple as a tested key, fresh batteries, and a family member who knows the backup code. For a hotel, office, school, clinic, warehouse, or multi-site rental operation, the plan needs named owners, offline credentials, guest or staff workarounds, and a record of what happened while the system was degraded.

## Classify Doors by Operational Impact

Start by sorting doors by consequence. A server room, medication room, staff entrance, guest unit, emergency exit, and low-value storage closet should not receive the same recovery design. Some doors must never prevent exit. Some must preserve audit records. Others can wait for normal maintenance.

| Door type | Recovery expectation | Practical fallback |
|---|---|---|
| Life safety and egress | Immediate | Code-compliant exit hardware, no blocked escape path |
| Critical operations | Minutes to 1 hour | Offline credential, key control, or staffed override |
| Revenue doors | Same shift | Backup guest code, local manager, or temporary key issue |
| Convenience doors | Same day | Manual process or delayed repair |
| Low-risk storage | Next business day | Maintenance ticket and access review |

This classification keeps the plan realistic. Redundant infrastructure on every door is expensive and often unnecessary. A single missing fallback on one critical door is the bigger risk.

## Define What Degraded Operation Means

Recovery targets should be written in plain operational language. "Ninety-nine percent uptime" does not help a front desk at midnight. A better target is: guest room access must be restored within 30 minutes using a backup code or manager-held key. A server room target might be: two authorized override holders must be reachable at all times, and any manual entry must be logged before the end of the shift.

Access logs also need a recovery target. If logs are cloud-only, confirm retention, export options, and what happens during outages. During a degraded period, keep a manual incident record with the time, door, person, approver, and reason for entry. That record may matter later for insurance, compliance, guest disputes, or internal investigation.

## Build Non-Cloud Access Paths

Every important door needs at least one path that does not depend on the same cloud service as daily operation. Depending on the site, that could be a physical key, offline PIN, local badge controller, emergency power terminal, local security desk, or mechanical override.

Physical keys still have a place in continuity planning. They should be labeled by door, stored securely, limited to authorized people, and audited. A key tossed in a drawer creates a different risk; a controlled key with a check-out process is a fallback. Rental and hotel operations should also keep guest fallback separate from owner or admin fallback, so a temporary access problem does not expose a master credential.

## Write Short Failure Procedures

The procedures should be short enough to use under stress. Cover the common failures first: lock battery dead, hub offline, internet down, vendor cloud outage, admin phone lost, staff member must be removed during an outage, and a door is reporting the wrong state.

Each procedure should name the person responsible, the fallback credential or device, the communication channel, the escalation contact, and the cleanup step after service returns. Cleanup matters because temporary codes, manual keys, and emergency permissions often outlive the incident if no one is assigned to remove them.

## Test the Plan Before It Is Needed

Disaster recovery that has never been tested is mostly a hope. Run practical tests during low-risk windows. Remove a lock from the network, simulate a dead battery, disable a hub, verify an offline code, open the controlled key box, and confirm someone can recover admin access without the usual phone.

For businesses, a quarterly tabletop review and at least one hands-on access recovery test per year is a reasonable baseline. After each test, update the procedure while the confusion is fresh. The goal is not a perfect drill; it is finding the unclear step before a guest, tenant, employee, or responder is waiting at the door.

## Compliance and Evidence

Standards such as ISO 22301 and NIST contingency planning emphasize documented procedures, assigned responsibility, testing, review, and improvement. A smart lock plan does not need inflated standards language to be useful. It needs evidence that critical access continues during predictable failures.

Keep current door lists, active credential exports, backup access instructions, incident logs, audit-log retention notes, and proof of periodic review. If a lock failure affects regulated areas, revenue operations, or more than one site, bring in a qualified access-control professional rather than stretching consumer smart lock habits into enterprise risk.
