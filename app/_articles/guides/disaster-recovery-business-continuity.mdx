## Quick Answer

A smart lock disaster recovery plan should answer four questions before anything fails: who can still enter, which doors must keep operating, how long the business can tolerate degraded access, and how access logs and credentials will be recovered. For homes this may be a backup key and fresh batteries. For offices, schools, hotels, healthcare, and multi-site facilities, it needs documented roles, failover paths, tested procedures, and offline access rules.

Do not confuse "we have an app" with continuity. Cloud access, hubs, Wi-Fi, batteries, staff permissions, vendor support, and physical keys can all fail independently.

## Classify Doors by Business Impact

Start with doors, not technology. A lobby door, server room, medication room, guest unit, staff entrance, and emergency exit do not have the same recovery target.

Use a simple priority model:

| Door type | Recovery target | Backup expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Life safety and egress | Immediate | Must not block exit; local code controls |
| Critical operations | Minutes to 1 hour | Offline credential, key, or staffed override |
| Revenue doors | 1 to 4 hours | Guest or staff access workaround |
| Convenience doors | Same day | Manual process is acceptable |
| Low-risk storage | Next business day | Repair ticket may be enough |

This classification drives budget. Not every door needs redundant infrastructure, but every critical door needs a tested fallback.

## Define RTO and RPO in Plain Language

Recovery Time Objective is how long the door or access process can be degraded before the business is seriously harmed. Recovery Point Objective is how much access data, credential change history, or audit log loss is acceptable.

For access control, RTO is often more important than abstract uptime. A hotel cannot wait hours for guest-room access during check-in. A small office may tolerate manual key checkout for half a day. A regulated room may need both fast access and preserved logs.

Document targets in language operations teams understand:

- Front entrance: staffed manual entry within 15 minutes.
- Guest units: backup code or key within 30 minutes.
- Server room: two authorized override holders available at all times.
- Audit logs: export or backup daily, with incident notes if systems are offline.

## Map Failure Modes

List the ways access can fail and what still works in each case:

| Failure | What may stop working | What should remain |
|---|---|---|
| Lock battery failure | Keypad, motor, remote commands | Physical key or emergency power |
| Hub failure | Remote commands, schedules, logs | Existing local codes if stored on lock |
| Internet outage | Cloud changes and remote unlock | Local keypad, local hub functions |
| Phone/app outage | App unlock and user management | Keypad, keys, admin backup account |
| Vendor cloud outage | Remote management | Local credentials and physical access |
| Staff offboarding failure | Revocation process | Emergency admin and audit review |

The plan is weak anywhere "nothing works" appears in the right column.

## Design Backup Access

Every critical door needs at least one non-cloud path. That may be a physical key, offline PIN stored on the lock, local badge controller, emergency power terminal, staffed security desk, or mechanical override. The right answer depends on the site, but it must be tested.

Physical keys still matter. Store them securely, label them by door, restrict who can use them, and audit them. A key in a drawer is not a continuity plan unless people know where it is and who can authorize its use.

For rentals and hotels, keep guest-facing fallback separate from administrator fallback. A guest backup code should not expose the master code or app owner account.

## Back Up Configuration and Logs

For small deployments, document lock model, door name, admin owner, battery type, backup key location, hub, Wi-Fi network, and emergency contact. For larger deployments, export user lists, door groups, access schedules, firmware versions, hub configuration, and audit logs on a schedule.

Audit logs may matter after an incident, insurance claim, compliance review, or guest dispute. If logs are cloud-only, understand the vendor's retention period and export options. During an outage, keep a manual incident log: who entered, who approved access, and when normal logging resumed.

## Create Response Procedures

A good DR plan is short enough to use during stress. Write procedures for common scenarios:

1. Lock battery dead at critical door.
2. Hub offline for one floor or building.
3. Internet down but local locks still work.
4. Vendor cloud outage.
5. Lost admin phone.
6. Employee or contractor access must be revoked during system outage.

Each procedure should name the owner, backup credential, communication channel, escalation contact, and post-incident cleanup.

## Test Before You Need It

Test recovery during planned windows. Remove a lock from the network, simulate a dead battery, disable a hub, test an offline code, verify the backup key, and confirm someone can recover admin access without the usual phone.

For businesses, run a quarterly tabletop exercise and at least one practical test per year. After each test, fix the documentation. The goal is not a perfect drill; it is finding the confusing step while no one is locked out.

## Align With Compliance

ISO 22301 and NIST contingency planning both expect documented procedures, exercise, review, and continuous improvement. You do not need to over-label every smart lock process with standards language, but you should be able to show that critical access functions have a continuity plan and that the plan is tested.

For HIPAA, SOC 2, insurance, hospitality, and multifamily operations, focus on evidence: current access list, backup procedure, incident log, audit-log retention, and proof of periodic review.

## When to Escalate

Use professional access-control design when failure blocks life safety, regulated areas, revenue operations, or more than one site. Consumer smart lock habits do not scale cleanly into enterprise continuity.

Escalate immediately after repeated hub failures, unexplained credential loss, missing audit logs, ransomware concern, or any outage that forced uncontrolled manual entry.
