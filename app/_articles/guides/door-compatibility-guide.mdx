## Measure Before Choosing a Brand

A smart lock can have excellent software and still be wrong for the door. Most replacement smart deadbolts are built for a standard cylindrical deadbolt, not every lock body. Mortise locks, multi-point doors, Euro cylinders, narrow stile doors, thick metal doors, glass near the lock, and older small-bore doors need extra checking before purchase.

For a quick pass/fail screen, use the [Door Compatibility Checker](/calculators/compatibility). Then use the measurements below to understand what needs attention.

## Quick Compatibility Decision

Use this page as a hub before choosing a model. A lock is a good fit only when the physical door prep, current lock type, clearance, access workflow, and installation rules all line up.

| Door situation | Usual smart lock path | What to verify first |
|---|---|---|
| Standard cylindrical deadbolt | Full replacement or retrofit | Thickness, backset, 2-1/8 inch bore, latch bore, interior clearance |
| Apartment or rental door | Interior retrofit first | Lease rules, exterior appearance, original key access, no-drill mounting |
| Mortise or multi-point door | Lock-type-specific hardware | Case size, handle set, cylinder format, installer availability |
| Metal, fire-rated, or commercial door | Professional review | Fire label, egress function, listing, drilling restrictions |
| Door with glass, trim, or storm door | Clearance-sensitive shortlist | Interior thumb-turn space, exterior trim, glass distance, storm-door gap |

If one physical measurement fails, do not assume software features can compensate. Use the [Installation Cost Estimator](/calculators/installation-cost) before accepting drilling, adapter plates, or locksmith work.

## The Measurements That Matter

Start with door thickness. Measure the flat door face at the lock location, not the beveled edge or raised panel. Many residential deadbolt locks fit common 1-3/8 inch to 1-3/4 inch doors, but thick doors may need extension kits and thin hollow doors may not support a proper deadbolt.

Next, measure backset: the distance from the door edge to the center of the deadbolt hole. The common North American sizes are 2-3/8 inch and 2-3/4 inch. If the backset is wrong, the latch will not meet the strike plate cleanly, and the lock may grind or fail auto-lock tests even if it appears installed.

The cross bore is the large round hole through the door face. Many smart deadbolts expect a 2-1/8 inch bore. Older doors may have smaller holes that require an adapter plate or careful drilling. The edge bore is the hole in the door edge for the latch; unusual pockets or rectangular cases may indicate mortise hardware rather than a simple deadbolt.

## Measurement Checklist

Write the measurements down before opening brand comparison pages:

1. Door thickness at the lock location.
2. Backset from door edge to bore center.
3. Cross bore diameter through the door face.
4. Edge bore or latch pocket size.
5. Distance from the lock to trim, glass, handles, and storm doors.
6. Current lock type: cylindrical deadbolt, knob, lever, mortise, multi-point, or Euro cylinder.
7. Door material: wood, metal, fiberglass, composite, fire-rated, or glass-adjacent.
8. Door handing and swing direction if the lock requires handing setup.
9. Whether the deadbolt moves smoothly by hand.
10. Whether drilling or exterior appearance changes are allowed.

For a second pass, use the [Door Measurement Fit Checker](/calculators/door-fit) and keep the values consistent across calculator, installer quote, and product manual review.

## Identify the Existing Lock Type

A standard cylindrical deadbolt has a separate deadbolt above the knob or lever, a round exterior cylinder, and an interior thumb turn. It is the easiest replacement case and the target for most consumer smart locks.

A mortise lock has a rectangular case inside the door edge, often combining latch and deadbolt. A multi-point lock moves bolts at several points along the door. A Euro cylinder uses a profile cylinder common outside North America. These are not standard deadbolt replacements. Buy hardware made for that lock type or involve an installer.

## Lock Type Compatibility Matrix

| Existing hardware | Compatibility risk | Safer next step |
|---|---|---|
| Separate deadbolt above knob or lever | Low if measurements are standard | Compare full replacements and retrofits |
| Deadbolt integrated with handle set | Medium | Check trim clearance and whether the handle set must stay |
| Mortise case in door edge | High | Search for mortise-specific smart hardware or use an installer |
| Multi-point lock | High | Use manufacturer-approved hardware only |
| Euro cylinder | Medium to high | Compare European retrofit locks and cylinder requirements |
| Narrow stile door | High | Confirm commercial narrow-stile hardware compatibility |

## Retrofit or Full Replacement

Retrofit locks keep the exterior hardware and replace only the interior thumb turn. They are useful for apartments, HOA restrictions, historic doors, and renters who cannot change the outside appearance. They still require the existing deadbolt to move smoothly by hand.

Full replacement locks replace both exterior and interior hardware. They are better when you want a keypad, fingerprint reader, new finish, or a worn deadbolt replaced. They require more clearance checking because the exterior and interior assemblies must cover the bore cleanly and avoid trim, glass, handles, and storm doors.

Choose retrofit when reversibility, exterior appearance, or lease risk matters most. Choose full replacement when the current hardware is worn, you need an exterior keypad, or you want a new deadbolt and credential system in one package. If you are unsure, compare the retrofit path with the [Retrofit Advisor](/calculators/retrofit-advisor).

## Special Door Conditions

Metal doors, fire-rated doors, commercial doors, and doors with glass near the lock deserve extra care. Drilling may require better tools, fire labels may restrict hardware changes, and glass can create security or clearance issues. Double doors need a stable inactive leaf before a smart deadbolt makes sense. Storm doors may not leave enough space for a bulky interior assembly.

For rentals, check lease and exterior appearance rules before installing visible hardware. Interior-only retrofits are often easier to justify, but they still must not damage the door or interfere with emergency exit.

## Common Failure Cases

The most common failed installs are not app problems. They usually start with the door:

- The bore is too small for a replacement deadbolt.
- The backset does not match the latch.
- The strike plate is misaligned, so the motor strains.
- The interior assembly hits trim, glass, blinds, or a storm door.
- A mortise or multi-point lock is mistaken for a standard deadbolt.
- The door is fire-rated or commercial, and hardware changes need approval.
- A renter replaces exterior hardware without permission.
- The lock fits physically but the signal path is weak because the door is metal or far from the hub.

After physical fit passes, use the [Signal Strength Calculator](/calculators/signal-strength) and [Protocol Selection Wizard](/calculators/protocol-wizard) so the lock is not compatible on paper but unreliable in daily use.

## When to Use a Professional

DIY installation is reasonable for a normal cylindrical deadbolt with standard measurements and a smooth existing bolt. Use a locksmith or installer for mortise locks, multi-point doors, metal security doors, commercial or fire-rated openings, doors with glass near the lock, or any installation that needs new holes. A poor fit creates both security and reliability problems.

Professional review is also useful when the lock is part of a rental, multifamily, hotel, office, clinic, school, or regulated building. In those cases, the lock affects operations, emergency access, insurance, privacy, and maintenance, not just convenience.
