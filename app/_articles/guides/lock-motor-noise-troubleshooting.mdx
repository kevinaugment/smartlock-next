## Compare Door-Open and Door-Closed Sound

A healthy smart lock makes a short, smooth motor sound and one final stop or click. Grinding, repeated clicking, slow straining, or squealing usually means the motor is fighting friction. The fastest diagnosis is to run the lock with the door open, then repeat with the door closed.

If the lock is quiet with the door open but loud with the door closed, the motor is probably fine. The bolt is rubbing the strike plate, the door has shifted, or weatherstripping is pushing the door out of position. If the noise happens even with the door open, look at batteries, mounting tension, debris, lubrication, or internal gear wear.

Do not keep forcing a noisy lock. Motor strain can turn a small alignment issue into gear wear, battery drain, and eventual failure.

## Fix Alignment Before Blaming the Motor

Most loud operation comes from a bolt that does not enter the strike opening cleanly. Look for shiny rub marks on the bolt or strike plate. Close the door slowly and watch whether the bolt lines up with the hole. Tighten loose hinge screws first; a slightly sagging door can be enough to make the motor sound rough.

If the alignment is close but still rubbing, adjust the strike plate or carefully enlarge the strike opening. After the fix, the lock should sound almost the same with the door closed as it does with the door open.

## Check Power and Mounting Pressure

Weak batteries can make a normal motor sound tired. Replace all cells with fresh batteries from the same pack before assuming internal damage. If the sound improves briefly and returns, the lock is still working too hard against friction.

Overtightened mounting screws can also pinch the mechanism. Loosen the interior screws a quarter turn and test again. The lock should be secure, but the body should not be compressed so tightly that the tailpiece or gear train binds.

## Lubricate Carefully

Use dry graphite or a lock-safe dry lubricant on bolt and latch contact points when the mechanism is dry or squeaky. Avoid heavy oils inside the lock body or keyway because they attract dust and can gum up over time. Do not spray lubricant into the motor housing unless the manufacturer specifically recommends it.

Fresh paint, wood dust, metal filings, and loose screws can also create scraping or clicking. If the door was recently painted or repaired, inspect the bolt path and strike area before replacing parts.

## When Noise Means Wear

If the lock is loud with the door open, has fresh batteries, is not overtightened, and still clicks or skips under no load, the gears or motor may be worn. This is more likely on high-traffic doors or locks that spent months fighting a bad strike plate.

Check warranty status and record a short video of the door-open test. For older consumer locks, replacement may be more practical than motor repair, especially if noise comes with failed cycles or fast battery drain.
