Choosing a smart lock protocol is less about picking the newest radio and more about deciding what should still work when the internet is slow, the batteries are aging, or the building has difficult walls. Wi-Fi, Zigbee, Z-Wave, Thread, Matter, and Bluetooth can all be good choices, but they fail in different ways.

For a single front door, Wi-Fi is often the simplest answer because it connects directly to the router and usually needs no separate hub. For a larger home, rental portfolio, or commercial building, low-power mesh protocols usually make more sense because they reduce battery drain and give the lock a more stable local path back to a controller. Matter adds useful cross-platform compatibility, but it does not erase the need to check which radio the lock uses and which features your platform exposes.

## The Short Version

| Priority | Best starting point | Main tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| Simple setup with no hub | Wi-Fi | Shorter battery life and more cloud dependence |
| Long battery life | Zigbee or Z-Wave | Requires a hub or controller |
| Thick walls or larger buildings | Z-Wave | Fewer consumer ecosystem choices |
| Apple, Google, Alexa, and SmartThings flexibility | Matter over Thread | Feature support still varies by platform |
| Phone-near-door access only | Bluetooth | Poor for remote management |

The cleanest rule is this: choose Wi-Fi when convenience matters more than battery life, choose Zigbee or Z-Wave when reliability and maintenance matter more than setup speed, and choose Matter when cross-platform ownership matters enough that you are willing to verify feature support carefully.

## Wi-Fi Locks

Wi-Fi locks connect through the home router, which is why they are so easy to install. There is no hub to buy, no mesh to plan, and no controller terminology to learn. For many homeowners and Airbnb hosts, that simplicity is the point.

The cost is power. A Wi-Fi lock has to wake often enough to remain reachable through the app or cloud service. That usually means battery changes measured in months, not years. If the lock sits far from the router, it may retransmit messages, wake longer, and drain batteries even faster.

Wi-Fi is a good fit when you have one or two locks, a strong 2.4 GHz signal at the door, and a workflow that benefits from quick remote code management. It is weaker for large buildings, low-maintenance rentals, or doors where internet outages must not interrupt the basic access workflow. For those cases, compare battery expectations in the [Battery Life Calculator](/calculators/battery-life) before buying.

## Zigbee Locks

Zigbee is a low-power mesh protocol. Instead of asking the lock to behave like a small Wi-Fi device, Zigbee lets the lock sleep most of the time and exchange short messages through a hub and nearby powered Zigbee devices. That is why Zigbee locks usually last much longer on the same battery size.

The catch is network quality. A Zigbee lock should not be the only device hanging at the edge of the network. It works best when powered routers, such as plugs or switches, create a stable path between the lock and hub. Battery-powered sensors usually do not repeat messages for the lock, so do not count them as infrastructure.

Choose Zigbee if you already use SmartThings, Hubitat, Home Assistant, Aqara, or another Zigbee-capable setup and you are comfortable checking link quality. It is especially attractive when battery life matters and the building is not hostile to 2.4 GHz radio signals.

## Z-Wave Locks

Z-Wave is also a low-power mesh protocol, but it uses sub-GHz frequencies in many markets. In practical terms, that often helps through walls, floors, and building materials that make 2.4 GHz networks unreliable. Z-Wave also has a long history in access control and property management deployments.

For multi-door sites, Z-Wave's biggest advantage is predictability. You can plan repeaters, controller placement, and secure inclusion as part of a deployment process instead of treating each lock as a standalone gadget. That matters for offices, multifamily buildings, and properties where a failed lock creates a support ticket.

The limitation is ecosystem breadth. Z-Wave is not as visible in consumer app ecosystems as Wi-Fi or Matter, and it usually assumes a more deliberate controller setup. For large homes, thick walls, or commercial planning, that tradeoff is often worth it. The [RF Coverage Estimator](/calculators/rf-coverage) is useful before standardizing on a Z-Wave model.

## Thread and Matter

Matter is not a radio protocol by itself. It is an interoperability standard that can run over Thread, Wi-Fi, or Ethernet. For battery-powered smart locks, the interesting version is usually Matter over Thread because Thread is designed for low-power mesh devices.

The benefit is multi-admin control. A Matter lock can be shared across supported ecosystems without choosing one platform forever at purchase time. That is valuable in mixed Apple, Google, Alexa, and SmartThings homes.

The careful part is feature parity. Basic lock and unlock behavior may work across platforms, while advanced functions such as guest code management, logs, schedules, Home Key, or vacation-rental automation may still depend on the lock vendor app or a specific ecosystem. Before buying, confirm the exact features you need in the platform you will actually use. The [Smart Home Integration Checker](/calculators/compatibility) helps catch those gaps early.

## Bluetooth Locks

Bluetooth is best understood as a local access method, not a full remote management strategy. It can be efficient and simple when the user is standing near the door, but it does not solve remote unlocks, guest scheduling, or fleet management unless the lock also has Wi-Fi, a bridge, or another hub.

Bluetooth-only locks can still be reasonable for interior rooms, low-risk doors, or rental situations where the owner wants a retrofit device without wiring or network planning. They are not ideal when you need reliable remote status, automatic code delivery, or multi-property operations.

## Security Differences

Modern smart lock security depends more on implementation and setup than on the protocol name alone. Z-Wave S2, Zigbee 3.0, Thread, Matter, and reputable Wi-Fi locks can all use strong encryption. The real questions are whether the lock uses secure pairing, whether firmware is maintained, whether user codes are managed properly, and whether audit logs are available when you need them.

If security is the deciding factor, start with [Are Smart Locks Actually Secure?](/articles/security/smart-lock-security-complete-analysis), then check whether the lock supports failed-attempt lockout, firmware updates, local fallback access, and clear credential controls. A strong protocol cannot compensate for weak PIN policy or poor operational habits.

## How to Choose

Start with the building, not the product page. Walk to the door and check signal conditions. Think about how often the lock will be used, who will manage codes, and what happens during an internet outage. A lock that works beautifully on a lab bench may be a poor choice for a metal door at the edge of a weak network.

For one home door, a Wi-Fi keypad lock is often the least complicated choice. For battery-sensitive homes, Zigbee or Thread can reduce maintenance. For thick walls and multi-door buildings, Z-Wave deserves serious consideration. For mixed smart-home ecosystems, Matter over Thread is promising, provided the exact model supports the features you need.

If you are buying for a rental or commercial environment, do not stop at protocol selection. Compare operating workflow in [Smart Locks for Airbnb](/articles/use-cases/smart-locks-airbnb-complete-guide), [Long-Term Rental Strategy](/articles/use-cases/long-term-rental-strategy), or [Enterprise Smart Lock Deployment](/articles/use-cases/enterprise-commercial-deployment). In those settings, guest codes, battery alerts, support procedures, and auditability usually matter more than the radio label on the box.

## Final Recommendation

Pick Wi-Fi for simple single-door convenience. Pick Zigbee or Z-Wave for lower battery maintenance and stronger local infrastructure. Pick Matter over Thread when interoperability is a real requirement and you have confirmed feature support. Avoid buying any smart lock until you have checked door fit, signal quality, backup access, and battery expectations.

That process takes longer than comparing spec sheets, but it prevents the most common mistake: buying the lock with the most familiar app instead of the one that fits the door, network, and access workflow.
