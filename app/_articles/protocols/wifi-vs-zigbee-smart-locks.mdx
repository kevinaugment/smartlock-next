Wi-Fi is the simple answer when the lock is for one door and the buyer wants remote control without planning a hub. Zigbee is the better answer when the lock is part of a managed smart home or rental setup where battery life, repeatability, and local mesh behavior matter more than the shortest installation path.

The practical question is not whether Wi-Fi or Zigbee is "better." It is who will own the network around the lock. If a homeowner controls the router, can replace batteries, and only needs one keypad, Wi-Fi is often fine. If a landlord, installer, or smart home owner already controls the hub and nearby repeaters, Zigbee can reduce battery drain and keep lock traffic away from a crowded Wi-Fi network.

## When Wi-Fi Makes Sense

Wi-Fi locks are popular because they remove the most obvious buying friction. The lock can usually connect to a 2.4 GHz network, use the brand app for remote access, and avoid a separate hub. For a nearby Airbnb host, a condo owner, or a single back door, that convenience may be worth the tradeoff.

The tradeoff is maintenance. Wi-Fi radios draw more power than low-power mesh radios, and weak signal at the door can cause retries, delayed status updates, and shorter battery life. A router that works perfectly for phones in the kitchen may still be marginal for a lock mounted on an exterior door. Before choosing Wi-Fi for a rental or high-use entry, estimate battery visits with the [Battery Life Calculator](/calculators/battery-life), then price those visits honestly.

## When Zigbee Makes Sense

Zigbee is stronger when the buyer accepts hub planning and wants a low-power mesh. A good Zigbee network uses powered devices as repeaters, keeps battery devices sleeping most of the time, and gives the owner more control over the lock's surrounding infrastructure. That can work well in Home Assistant, SmartThings, Hubitat, or other hub-centered homes.

The important word is "good." A poor Zigbee mesh can be worse than a simple Wi-Fi lock because the owner now has a hub, repeaters, pairing behavior, and device support to maintain. Put a stable powered repeater near the door, confirm the exact lock model is supported by the chosen hub, and test lock, unlock, status, battery reporting, and code management before trusting it on an exterior or guest-facing door.

## Rental Decision

For rentals, the decision often comes down to ownership. If the tenant controls the router and the landlord does not want to manage a hub inside the unit, Wi-Fi may be the cleaner choice even if batteries need more attention. If the landlord owns the hub, repeaters, and lock administration, Zigbee can make more sense because the access workflow is less dependent on a tenant's network choices.

Remote rentals deserve extra caution. Every low-battery alert, failed code update, and lockout message becomes labor. A Wi-Fi lock may be acceptable for one local property, but it can become expensive when a property is far away or heavily used by guests and cleaners. Zigbee can reduce that burden only when the hub and mesh are as standardized as the lock itself.

Apartment owners should also think about support boundaries. If the resident is building a personal smart home and already has a Zigbee network, a Zigbee lock may fit naturally. If the resident only wants a keypad and app control, Wi-Fi is easier to explain and easier to remove later. For multifamily rollouts, pilot several real doors before standardizing because a mesh that works in one unit may not behave the same across floors, corridors, and construction types.

Choose Wi-Fi when the setup needs to be simple and the maintenance risk is low. Choose Zigbee when the lock will live inside a controlled hub ecosystem and the owner can design the mesh around the door. The wrong choice is buying Zigbee without mesh discipline or buying Wi-Fi while pretending battery visits are free.
