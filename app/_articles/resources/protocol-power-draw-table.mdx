Smart lock battery life is mostly about how often the radio wakes up, how long it stays awake, and how hard the motor works. Battery brand matters, but protocol choice, signal quality, polling behavior, firmware, temperature, and door alignment usually explain the big differences between locks.

Wi-Fi locks often drain faster because they have more connection overhead. A Wi-Fi radio may need to wake, scan, authenticate, obtain or refresh network state, negotiate encrypted traffic, send data, and close the session. Low-power mesh protocols such as Zigbee, Z-Wave, and Thread are designed around short, sleepy device events. Bluetooth LE can be very efficient for local access, but it usually needs a bridge or phone nearby for remote features.

Use the [Protocol Selection Wizard](/calculators/protocol-wizard) before choosing a lock for a rental, cold climate, or high-use door. A protocol that is acceptable for one nearby front door can become a maintenance problem across 30 units.

## Why Wake Time Matters More Than Marketing

The radio's active current is only part of the story. Time awake is often the larger factor. A low-power mesh lock may wake briefly to report that it locked or unlocked, then return to sleep. A Wi-Fi lock may remain awake much longer while it reconnects to the network and cloud service. If the signal is weak, that awake time can stretch further because the lock retries.

That is why two owners can report very different battery life for the same model. The lock near a strong router or well-placed hub sleeps quickly. The lock on a metal door, far from the access point, wakes repeatedly and spends more time recovering from failed communication.

## Protocol Behavior in Plain Terms

Wi-Fi is convenient because it often avoids a hub, but it usually pays for that convenience in battery life. It can be the right choice for a single owner-occupied door or a nearby Airbnb host, but it needs strong 2.4 GHz signal and a realistic battery replacement plan.

Zigbee and Thread use low-power 2.4 GHz radios. They can deliver strong battery life when the mesh is healthy, but they still suffer from interference, weak repeaters, bad hub placement, and crowded channels. Thread adds the Matter path and IP-based architecture, but a Matter label does not remove the need for a good border router.

Z-Wave is also low power and often benefits from sub-GHz propagation, which can help through walls and corridors. It still needs a compatible hub and enough powered devices in the right places. Bluetooth LE is efficient for local presence and phone-based control, but remote access depends on bridge design.

## Batteries and Voltage Curves

Smart locks are sensitive to voltage, not just remaining capacity. Alkaline AA batteries have a declining voltage curve, so cold weather or heavy motor load can make the lock report low battery before the cells are fully empty. Lithium AA batteries often perform better in cold conditions and maintain voltage more consistently, but owners should follow the lock manufacturer's recommendations.

Rechargeable batteries are tricky. Many NiMH cells run at a lower nominal voltage, which can trigger early low-battery warnings. Some rechargeable lithium AA products hold a flat output until they drop suddenly, which can make the lock report full battery for a long time and then fail quickly. That may be acceptable for a monitored home, but it is risky for a remote rental.

## When the Hub Causes the Problem

A lock can drain quickly even when the radio protocol is efficient. Aggressive polling, buggy drivers, repeated failed status requests, and automations that wake the lock too often can destroy battery life. If batteries die in days rather than months, inspect the hub logs and polling behavior before replacing hardware.

Door mechanics also matter. If the deadbolt rubs, the motor draws more current on every lock cycle. Before blaming Wi-Fi, Zigbee, Z-Wave, or Thread, test whether the bolt throws smoothly by hand with the door open and closed.

The practical battery strategy is simple: choose a low-power protocol when door count or distance makes battery visits expensive, place the hub or repeater correctly, use the recommended battery chemistry, avoid unnecessary polling, and fix mechanical drag before it becomes an electronics problem.
