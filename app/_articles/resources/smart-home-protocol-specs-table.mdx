Smart lock protocols should be judged by the door, not by a spec sheet alone. Frequency, mesh design, hub requirements, power use, security model, and ecosystem support all matter, but the best protocol is the one that stays reliable at the actual door with the actual users and maintenance team.

The main choices are Wi-Fi, Bluetooth LE, Zigbee, Z-Wave, Thread, and Matter. Matter is different from the others because it is an interoperability layer rather than a radio by itself. A Matter smart lock may use Thread or Wi-Fi underneath, and that transport affects battery life, hub requirements, and network planning.

Use the [Protocol Selection Wizard](/calculators/protocol-wizard) first if you are choosing hardware, then validate the physical layout with the [Mesh Topology Guide](/articles/resources/mesh-topology-guide) or the relevant signal tools.

## Frequency and Range

Z-Wave often performs well for locks because it operates below the crowded 2.4 GHz band in many regions. Lower-frequency signals can be more forgiving through walls, people, and dense construction. That makes Z-Wave a practical choice for apartments, older homes, and managed properties where the hub path can be controlled.

Zigbee and Thread use 2.4 GHz radios. They can work very well, but they share spectrum with Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, microwaves, and other devices. Their reliability depends heavily on channel choice, repeater quality, and coordinator or border-router placement. A good 2.4 GHz mesh can be solid; a careless one can create battery and status problems.

Wi-Fi uses higher power and familiar infrastructure. It is convenient because many Wi-Fi locks avoid a separate hub, but the lock still needs strong 2.4 GHz coverage at the door. A fast router in the living room does not guarantee a stable lock on an exterior door.

Bluetooth LE is efficient and useful for nearby phone access, but remote control usually depends on a bridge, hub, or platform device. It is better understood as a local access layer than a complete remote-management architecture.

## Mesh Design and Hubs

Battery locks are usually sleepy devices. They should not be counted on to repeat traffic for other devices. The mesh is built by powered routers, plugs, modules, switches, border routers, or hubs placed between the controller and the door.

Z-Wave and Zigbee networks depend on a controller. Thread uses border routers and IP routing, which can reduce some single-controller assumptions but still requires a healthy Thread network. Wi-Fi is usually a star topology around the router or access points. None of these architectures removes the need to test the real installed location.

## Matter and Interoperability

Matter can reduce platform lock-in by giving Apple Home, Google Home, Alexa, SmartThings, and Home Assistant a more common device language. For smart locks, that is useful, but it does not guarantee that every advanced feature appears in every app. Guest codes, logs, schedules, Home Key style features, and battery details may still vary by model and ecosystem.

The key question is whether the lock is Matter over Thread or Matter over Wi-Fi. Thread usually fits low-power battery devices better. Wi-Fi may be simpler for buyers who already have strong coverage, but it carries the same battery and signal concerns as other Wi-Fi locks.

## Which Should You Choose?

Choose Z-Wave when the building is difficult, the door count is growing, and a mature hub workflow is acceptable. Choose Zigbee when you already maintain a strong Zigbee mesh and the lock model is well supported. Choose Thread when Matter interoperability and low-power mesh behavior are both priorities. Choose Wi-Fi when one-door simplicity matters more than battery optimization. Choose Bluetooth LE when local phone access is the main use case and remote management is secondary.

Do not choose from protocol names alone. Confirm the exact lock, hub, app, feature set, credential workflow, and door location. The right protocol is the one that preserves access after the first month, not the one with the most impressive specification line.
