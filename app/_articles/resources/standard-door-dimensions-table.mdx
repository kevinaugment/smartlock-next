Smart locks are often sold as easy replacements, but they still depend on the door being prepared like a standard deadbolt door. Before buying, measure the backset, bore size, door thickness, stile width, latch faceplate, and strike alignment. A lock that fits the app and budget can still fail if the door geometry is wrong.

Use [Door Compatibility Guide](/articles/guides/door-compatibility-guide) before ordering hardware, especially on older homes, apartments, metal doors, narrow-stile doors, and decorative entry doors. Guessing from product photos is how buyers end up drilling twice.

## The Measurements That Matter

Backset is the distance from the edge of the door to the center of the bore hole. The common deadbolt backsets are 2-3/8 inches and 2-3/4 inches. Many replacement latches adjust to both, but not every lock does. If the backset is wrong, the latch will not meet the strike correctly.

Door thickness is the next hard stop. Many residential doors are around 1-3/8 to 1-3/4 inches thick, while commercial or custom doors can be thicker. If the door is too thick, the screws or spindle may be too short without a thick-door kit. If it is too thin, the lock may not clamp securely.

The cross bore is the large round hole through the face of the door. Many modern deadbolts use a 2-1/8 inch bore. Older doors may have smaller bores that worked for mechanical deadbolts but do not leave enough room for a bulky smart lock body. Enlarging a bore is possible, but it should be done with the right jig so the new hole stays centered.

Stile width matters on doors with glass, panels, or narrow frames. The outside and inside halves of a smart lock are wider than many mechanical deadbolts. If there is not enough flat surface around the bore, the lock may hit trim, glass stops, raised panels, or weatherstripping.

## Latch and Strike Details

The latch faceplate can be round-corner, square-corner, or drive-in. A mismatch can often be solved with light carpentry, but it should not be ignored. The strike plate also needs enough depth for the deadbolt to extend fully. A shallow strike pocket can make the lock report jammed even when the door appears closed.

If the door has two holes, check the center-to-center spacing between the handle and deadbolt. One-piece connected locksets require specific spacing. If the spacing is wrong, use a separate deadbolt and handle rather than forcing an integrated unit.

Handing is the direction the door swings and which side the lock must throw. Many modern smart locks learn handing during setup, but some still need a physical switch or app selection. If the lock moves the wrong direction during calibration, stop and confirm handing before repeated motor cycles drain batteries or stress the latch.

## Older and Non-Standard Doors

Older doors often create the most surprises. Smaller bores, unusual backsets, thick decorative slabs, and worn frames can all turn a simple install into a carpentry job. Mortise locks are a separate category: if the door edge has a large rectangular lock case rather than a simple tubular latch, a standard deadbolt-style smart lock usually will not fit.

Metal security doors, gates, patio doors, and narrow-stile commercial doors also need caution. Some are too thin for standard hardware, some need spacers, and some require specialty locks. Do not assume a consumer deadbolt will fit just because there is already a key cylinder.

## Before You Drill

Before modifying the door, confirm the lock model's supported backset, bore size, door thickness range, latch faceplate options, and thick-door kit availability. Check whether the door is fire-rated, part of a rental unit, governed by an HOA, or subject to commercial code. Those constraints can matter more than the measurements.

The best pre-purchase test is physical. Measure twice, inspect the strike, test the deadbolt by hand with the door open and closed, and only then order the lock. A smart lock cannot compensate for a door that was never prepared for it.
