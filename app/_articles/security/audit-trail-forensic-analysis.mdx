Smart lock audit trails are useful only if they can answer a real question after something goes wrong: who had access, which credential was used, when the event happened, whether access was granted or denied, and whether the record can be trusted. A long export of vague entries is not an investigation system.

For homes, basic logs may be enough. For rentals, offices, healthcare, finance, schools, or regulated facilities, audit logging becomes part of access control, privacy compliance, and incident response. The goal is not to collect every possible signal forever. The goal is to keep the records needed to explain access events without creating a privacy problem.

Use the [Security Compliance Checker](/calculators/security-compliance) and [Privacy Compliance Checker](/calculators/privacy-compliance) before deploying logs in a business or rental setting.

## What A Useful Log Contains

Every access event should identify the door, timestamp, user or credential, event type, result, and source. A useful record says "Cleaner code unlocked Unit 204 at 10:14 AM from keypad." A weak record says "Access granted."

At minimum, log these fields:

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Door or lock name | Establishes the location |
| Timestamp and timezone | Builds a reliable timeline |
| User, role, or credential ID | Links the event to an assigned access method |
| Event type | Shows unlock, lock, failed PIN, code change, admin action, or battery alert |
| Result | Distinguishes granted, denied, timeout, jammed, or offline |
| Source | Shows keypad, app, hub, automation, API, or admin console |

Unique codes are essential. If five people share one cleaner code, the log can show that the cleaner code was used, but not which person used it. That may be acceptable for a home and unacceptable for a business.

## Retention And Privacy

Audit retention should match the risk and legal purpose. A vacation rental may need enough history to resolve guest disputes and support claims. A healthcare or financial facility may need longer retention under formal policy. A private home usually does not need years of detailed access history.

Longer retention increases privacy and disclosure risk. If logs include employees, tenants, patients, visitors, or guests, treat them as personal data. Define who can view logs, why they can view them, and when records are deleted or archived.

For privacy requirements, read [Smart Lock Data Privacy & Compliance](/articles/security/data-privacy-compliance-guide).

## Investigation Workflow

When investigating an access event, start by preserving the original logs. Export or screenshot the relevant period before changing users, deleting codes, or resetting the lock. Note the timezone and the system that produced the export.

Build a timeline around the incident. Include successful unlocks, failed PIN attempts, admin changes, code creation, code deletion, battery alerts, offline periods, and any camera or doorbell events. The timeline should separate confirmed facts from assumptions.

Then verify credential ownership. A code assigned to "maintenance" may not be enough if multiple people had it. Check whether the code was shared, expired, reused, or created for a different purpose.

## Anomaly Detection Without Overkill

Most teams do not need complex machine learning. They need alerts that match operational risk. Useful alerts include repeated failed PINs, access outside expected hours, admin changes, deleted logs, offline lock during a high-risk period, and codes used after a person should no longer have access.

For larger deployments, send logs to a central system or SIEM so security teams can compare door events with identity, HR, visitor, camera, and alarm data. The value comes from correlation, not from the lock log alone.

## Tamper Resistance

If logs may be used for disputes, insurance, compliance, or legal review, store them somewhere that normal lock admins cannot quietly edit. That may mean exporting to a property management system, SIEM, cloud storage with retention controls, or another system with access restrictions.

Document who exported the logs, when, from which system, and whether the export is complete. That chain of custody matters when the record needs to be trusted later.

## Final Recommendation

Set audit logging policy before the first incident. Use unique codes, keep enough history for your risk level, restrict log access, and test whether you can reconstruct a simple event. If you cannot answer "who, when, where, what result, and from which credential," the audit trail is not ready.
