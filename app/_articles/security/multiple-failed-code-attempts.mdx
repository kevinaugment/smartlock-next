Multiple failed code attempts usually mean one of two things: a legitimate user is confused, or someone is testing the lock. The right response is to preserve access for the right people while treating repeated unknown attempts as a security signal.

Most smart locks disable the keypad for a short period after several wrong PIN entries. The physical key, inside thumb turn, and sometimes the app may still work. The lockout is meant to slow guessing attacks without permanently locking out the owner.

## What Lockout Means

During keypad lockout, the lock ignores new keypad entries for a short period. Some locks beep, flash, or show a lockout message. The timer may last one minute, several minutes, or longer after repeated failures.

Do not keep pressing buttons during the lockout. Wait for the full timeout, then enter the correct code slowly. If you are outside and need access immediately, use the app, physical key, another authorized user, or the backup access plan.

For a general code failure path, see [Smart Lock Code Not Working](/articles/troubleshooting/smart-lock-code-not-working).

## Common Innocent Causes

Failed attempts often come from simple mistakes. A guest may have the wrong code, may not know whether to press a checkmark or lock button, or may be entering an expired code. A family member may remember an old PIN. Cold fingers, worn keypads, poor lighting, or a sticky button can also create repeated failures.

If the timing matches an expected arrival, contact the person before assuming an attack. Send the correct code and the exact keypad steps. For rentals, avoid long instructions; tell the guest where to enter the code, whether to wake the keypad, and what button completes entry.

## Suspicious Patterns

Treat attempts as suspicious when they happen late at night, repeat over multiple lockout cycles, use obvious codes such as 0000 or 1234, appear when nobody expected should be at the door, or line up with motion/video of an unknown person.

One burst of failed attempts may be a mistake. Repeated attempts after a lockout is more serious. If the lock is connected to a doorbell or camera, review the footage before clearing the alert.

## Immediate Response

If the attempts look suspicious, change active codes that could be guessed or shared. Review recent access logs, check whether any old users still have access, and inspect the door for tampering or damage. If video shows a clear attempt to break in, document it and consider a police report through the non-emergency channel.

Do not respond by creating one new shared code for everyone. That removes accountability. Instead, assign unique codes to each person or role and remove codes that are no longer needed.

## Prevent Future Problems

Use longer PINs if the lock supports them, avoid obvious patterns, and give every user a unique code. Enable failed-attempt alerts and lockout settings where available. Remove temporary codes promptly after guests, cleaners, contractors, or employees no longer need access.

For rentals and businesses, keep a simple access policy: who can create codes, when temporary codes expire, and who reviews failed-attempt alerts. [Secure Smart Lock Best Practices](/articles/security/secure-smart-lock-best-practices) covers the broader hardening routine.

## After The Lockout Clears

Once access is restored, test the correct code, confirm the lock reports normal status, and check whether the failed attempts were logged. If the keypad is physically unreliable, clean it gently and test multiple buttons. If valid codes keep failing, the issue may be code sync, keypad hardware, low battery, or firmware rather than security.

The goal is not to panic over every wrong PIN. The goal is to notice patterns, remove stale access, and keep a backup path ready when a legitimate user is locked out.
