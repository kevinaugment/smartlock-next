Smart locks can be secure, but the useful question is not whether every smart lock is safer than every mechanical lock. The useful question is whether a specific lock, installed on a specific door, with a specific access policy, reduces or increases your real risk.

A well-installed smart lock from a reputable manufacturer can add protections that a traditional lock does not have: unique user codes, auto-lock, access logs, failed-attempt lockout, remote revocation, and alerts. A poorly chosen or poorly managed smart lock can add new failure points: weak PINs, neglected firmware, bad door alignment, cloud dependence, and unclear backup access.

Use the [Security Scorecard](/calculators/offline-resilience) if you want a structured assessment. This guide explains the practical threat model so you can separate serious risks from dramatic but unlikely hacking stories.

## Methodology And Scope

This analysis reviews smart lock risk across five surfaces: physical entry, PIN and credential handling, wireless protocol security, vendor app or cloud account exposure, and operational recovery. The protocol review focuses on current lock-relevant security models such as Z-Wave S2 access-control classes, Zigbee 3.0 install-code and trust-center behavior, Thread and Matter commissioning, Bluetooth LE pairing, and Wi-Fi cloud-account dependency.

The vulnerability review is category-based rather than a maintained live database. We use public CVE patterns, vendor advisories, protocol documentation, and recurring field failure modes to identify the kinds of weaknesses buyers should check before deployment. Do not treat this page as a guarantee that one named model is vulnerability-free; use it as a threat-model checklist before reading a product manual, firmware changelog, and vendor support policy.

The practical scoring bias is intentional: common failures such as reused PINs, unremoved users, weak doors, dead batteries, and cloud account recovery gaps are weighted more heavily than rare remote attacks that require unusual proximity, equipment, or implementation defects.

## Smart Lock Security In Plain Terms

Most smart lock incidents are not movie-style remote hacks. The common problems are simpler: reused codes, shared master PINs, dead batteries, failed code removal, bad installation, weak account security, and guests or tenants keeping access longer than intended.

That does not mean digital security is irrelevant. Wireless encryption, secure pairing, firmware updates, and cloud account protection matter. But for most homes and rental properties, the largest gains come from basic operational discipline.

| Risk area | What usually matters most |
|---|---|
| Physical entry | Lock grade, door/frame strength, strike installation |
| PIN access | Unique codes, code length, lockout after failed attempts |
| Wireless access | Modern encrypted protocol, secure pairing, firmware support |
| App account | Strong password, MFA where available, phone security |
| Operations | Removing old users, battery policy, backup access, log review |

## Physical Security Still Comes First

A smart lock is still attached to a door. If the deadbolt, strike plate, screws, frame, or door alignment are weak, the app cannot fix the real security problem. For exterior doors, physical grade and installation quality should be reviewed before protocol features.

Look for a strong deadbolt, proper strike reinforcement, full bolt extension, and smooth motor operation. If the motor strains, the lock may fail to lock completely or drain batteries quickly. For door-fit checks, start with the [Door Compatibility Guide](/articles/guides/door-compatibility-guide) and [Door Alignment Guide](/articles/resources/door-alignment-guide).

Traditional picking and forced entry remain more likely than sophisticated wireless attacks for many residential doors. A smart lock should improve access control without distracting from basic door hardening.

## PIN Codes Are The Everyday Control

The keypad is often the most important security surface because it is used by real people every day. Short, reused, obvious, or shared codes weaken the lock no matter how strong the encryption is.

Use unique codes for each person or role. Avoid birthdays, addresses, repeated digits, and old default codes. Enable failed-attempt lockout if the lock supports it. Remove codes immediately when a guest, cleaner, contractor, tenant, or employee no longer needs access.

For homes, this may be a short list of family and trusted visitors. For rentals and commercial sites, code management becomes a process. Use [Guest Code Planner](/calculators/guest-code), [Change Master Code](/articles/guides/change-master-code), and [Share Access Securely](/articles/guides/share-access-securely) to avoid turning one convenient keypad into a shared secret.

## Wireless And Protocol Security

Modern protocols can be strong when implemented correctly. Z-Wave S2, Zigbee 3.0, Thread, Matter, and reputable Wi-Fi locks all support encrypted communication paths. The difference is less about one universal winner and more about secure pairing, key handling, certification, update history, and ecosystem support.

Z-Wave S2 is often respected in access-control contexts because locks use strong pairing and access-control security classes. Zigbee 3.0 can also be secure, but the quality of the hub, install-code process, and network management matters. Thread and Matter use a modern commissioning model, but you still need to verify which lock features are available in your platform. Wi-Fi locks depend heavily on the vendor cloud, app authentication, and your home network hygiene.

If protocol choice is central to your decision, compare [Wi-Fi vs Zigbee vs Z-Wave](/articles/protocols/smart-lock-protocols-overview) and [Zigbee vs Z-Wave](/articles/protocols/zigbee-vs-zwave-comparison). For security alone, do not buy a lock that has vague encryption claims, no visible firmware support, or unclear pairing instructions.

## Cloud And App Risk

Remote access is useful, but it changes the trust model. A cloud-connected lock depends on the vendor account, the phone app, backend availability, and notification reliability. That does not make it unsafe by default, but it means your security review must include the account and recovery process.

Use a strong account password and MFA where the vendor supports it. Keep the phone secure. Remove old phones and old admins from the account. Check whether lock and unlock still work locally if the internet is down. For rentals and businesses, document who owns the admin account and what happens when that person leaves.

Cloud outages are usually an operations problem before they are a security breach. The lock should still have a local way to unlock, and the team should know how to grant access if remote code creation is temporarily unavailable. The [Emergency Backup Planner](/calculators/emergency-backup) is useful for testing that process.

## Audit Logs Help, But They Are Not Magic

Access logs are one of the strongest practical benefits of smart locks. They can show when a code was used, when a door was locked, or when failed attempts occurred. That can help with guest disputes, maintenance questions, compliance reviews, or incident investigations.

Logs are only useful if they are retained long enough, tied to unique users, and reviewed when there is a reason. A log that says "Guest Code" was used is much weaker than a log tied to a specific reservation or role. A shared cleaner code used by five people has limited investigative value.

For regulated or commercial settings, pair this guide with [Audit Trail Forensic Analysis](/articles/security/audit-trail-forensic-analysis), [Data Privacy Compliance](/articles/security/data-privacy-compliance-guide), and the [Security Compliance Checker](/calculators/security-compliance).

## Practical Hardening Checklist

Use this checklist before relying on a smart lock for an exterior door, rental, or business:

1. Confirm the lock fits the door and the deadbolt extends smoothly.
2. Replace default master codes and remove unused users.
3. Use unique PINs for people, guests, cleaners, vendors, and admins.
4. Enable failed-attempt lockout and alerts where available.
5. Keep firmware and the mobile app updated.
6. Secure the vendor account with a strong password and MFA if supported.
7. Review battery alerts and define a replacement threshold.
8. Document backup access before the first emergency.
9. Review logs only for legitimate security or operational reasons.
10. Reassess access whenever a resident, employee, cleaner, or vendor changes.

## Final Verdict

A modern smart lock can be safer than a basic mechanical lock when it is installed correctly and managed well. It gives you unique credentials, revocation, logs, alerts, and automation that a traditional key cannot provide. It can also be worse than a good deadbolt if the door is weak, the code policy is sloppy, or the vendor account is neglected.

For most buyers, the best answer is not "avoid smart locks." It is "buy a reputable lock, harden the door, use unique codes, keep a backup path, and treat access management as part of security." That is the difference between a smart lock as a useful control and a smart lock as another unmanaged gadget.
